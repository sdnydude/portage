# OVERTONE

## A Design Philosophy

Every sounding body speaks twice: once in its fundamental, and again in the quiet arithmetic that rides above it. OVERTONE is a movement devoted to that second voice — the harmonic series as visual scripture. It treats the partials of a vibrating string the way a cartographer treats coastline: something real, measurable, and worth a lifetime of patient drafting. Work in this movement must read as documentation of an invisible physics, rendered by a hand at the absolute top of its field — every curve plotted, never sketched; every interval measured, never guessed.

Space is silence. The ground of any OVERTONE composition is a deep, warm near-black — the noise floor of a well-treated room — and it must dominate. Form enters the way sound enters silence: as thin, continuous, mathematically true lines whose amplitudes decay exactly as nature decays them, 1/n, partial by partial. Density accumulates only through patient repetition of these true curves, layered like a long exposure of a string settling. Nothing decorative is permitted; every mark is a datum. The result should feel meticulously crafted, the product of countless hours of calibration.

Color is calibration, not ornament. One warm signal color — the glow of tube bias, of a VU needle's home — carries the fundamental. One cool color — oscilloscope phosphor — carries the partials, stepping down in opacity as the series climbs. Both sit against the near-black floor with the restraint of instrument-panel design: light where information lives, darkness everywhere else. A hairline neutral, the grey of brushed faceplates, may rule and index. No other hues. The palette must look chosen by an engineer who has stared at signal for thirty years.

Scale and rhythm obey the monochord. Divisions of the canvas fall at harmonic ratios — the octave's half, the fifth's third, the fourth's quarter — so that even the margins are tuned. Reference markers, index numerals, and clinical micro-labels in a fixed-width hand may annotate the field, small and unwavering, as though the piece were a plate torn from an imaginary discipline's atlas. Typography beyond these labels is a single confident act: one name, set thin and wide and perfectly kerned, integrated into the field like an engraving on an instrument — never floating above it, never explaining it.

Composition balances the way a chord balances: weight left as attack, decay right as release. The eye should travel the way sound travels through time. Master-level execution is non-negotiable — alignments refined to the pixel, curves sampled densely enough to be glass-smooth, spacing labored over with painstaking attention until the whole plate feels inevitable. The finished object must look like proof: proof that something as ephemeral as consonance can be mapped, measured, and hung on a wall.
